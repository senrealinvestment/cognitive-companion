# Architecture literature — Cognitive Companion (companion-next)

> **Scope and safety.** This is a design literature review for simulation and shadow-workflow development only—not a clinical protocol, medical device claim, or live VCU care recommendation. The clinician remains the decision-maker; no automatic orders, treatment instructions, or dose generation are proposed here.

## Bottom line

The closest evidence supports a *bounded cognitive-aid system*, rather than an autonomous clinical agent. Perioperative emergency manuals/checklists and digital cognitive aids can improve process adherence in simulation, but their uptake, display design, training, and context matter.[3-6,38-39] Cognitive-forcing tools are plausible countermeasures to anchoring and premature closure, yet workplace diagnostic-debiasing evidence is heterogeneous and should be treated as a prompt to reassess—not a diagnostic adjudicator.[7-10] Clinical-CDSS literature also makes the safety case for two catalogs, prioritization, visible provenance, and faculty override: repeated or low-value alerts are overridden or ignored, while automation bias can induce errors of commission and omission.[11-17] The literature does **not** establish that glasses, ambient audio, RAG, or a control-tower-style consultant improve patient outcomes in this combined architecture. Simulation → shadow is therefore the proportionate development sequence.

## Prior pack (what 2026-09 already covered)

The 2026-09 evidence pack already documented the baseline content and interface literature: Arriaga’s simulated OR-crisis checklist trial [39]; Stanford emergency-manual implementation; cognitive forcing/debiasing theory; anesthesia auditory-display and head-worn-display (HWD) studies; pediatric AR/Glass simulations; and the HWD systematic review. It also documented the camera-free HUD choice and its limits. This review **extends rather than re-litigates** that pack: the emphasis here is architecture—catalog separation, matching versus evidence consultation, source gating, human override, ambient workflow, and tested cousins. The earlier result remains important: HWD evidence is largely gaze, detection, usability, or simulation evidence, not clinical-outcome evidence.[23]

### Interpretation of the table

This evidence supports **constraints**, not a claim that companion-next will improve outcomes. The strongest transferable claim is that a reviewed cognitive aid can support process adherence under simulated crisis conditions.[38-39] The architecture therefore should fail safely in ways paper manuals already do: if it cannot identify a reviewed aid, it should say so and become quiet rather than manufacture a plausible protocol.

## Similar architectures tried or postulated (named systems + what was measured)

### Implemented/evaluated cognitive-aid systems

**Stanford Emergency Manual (EM) / Stanford Anesthesia Cognitive Aid Program — implemented cognitive-aid content, not an AI matcher.** Stanford’s official site provides Version 4.4 downloads and describes 26 crises plus CRM points, with iterative simulation and clinical-use feedback; it also describes use after help has arrived or when a clinician can pause, often with a separate reader role.[1] Goldhaber-Fiebert and colleagues describe emergency manuals as context-relevant cognitive aids and implementation as a developing healthcare practice.[2] This is the closest content analogue for the EMERGENCY catalog: a reviewed, finite, visible artifact rather than generated medical prose. It does **not** validate automatic speech recognition, event matching, or HUD delivery.

**OR Crisis Checklists / Project Check (Ariadne) — evaluated in simulation.** Arriaga et al. used 17 OR teams across 106 simulated crises; missed lifesaving processes were lower with checklists (6% versus 23%).[39] Ariadne’s current primary project page describes an updated/digitized, locally editable checklist implementation resource.[34] The trial supports checklist content availability in simulation; it does not show patient outcome benefit, nor compare a full-page display against progressive disclosure.

**Perioperative bundle, paper, and mobile cognitive aids — interface/design evaluations.** van Haperen et al. compared two cognitive-aid bundle designs in simulated perioperative emergencies.[3] Watkins et al. compared paper and electronic versions of the Society for Pediatric Anesthesia emergency checklist in trainee simulation.[4] Clebone et al. reported development/usability testing of the *Pedi Crisis* mobile app,[5] and a later retrospective survey asked why critical-event checklists are not always used.[6] These are the closest cousins for CC’s “entire aid, rapid lookup, reader/faculty control” design questions. They indicate that format and adoption matter; they do not establish that one universal compact or drip interface is safer.

**PediAppRREST — tablet cognitive aid, randomized simulation trial.** Corazza et al. tested a tablet application for pediatric cardiac-arrest guideline adherence in a randomized trial and reported fewer guideline deviations than comparison conditions.[38] It is a direct digital-cognitive-aid cousin, but it remains a pediatric resuscitation simulation/application result—not evidence for general ICU, glasses, LLM matching, or clinical outcomes.

### Implemented/evaluated human-filtered and display architectures

**Anesthesiology Control Tower (ACTFAST) — human/telemedicine filter around OR CDSS.** ACTFAST feasibility work described a real-time telemedicine support system that combined remote monitoring with customized decision support.[18] ACTFAST-3 was a single-center pilot RCT designed to assess feasibility and two quality metrics; its own authors characterize it as a pilot toward an efficacy study.[19] In the subsequent TECTONICS randomized trial, telemedicine support did not improve the tested postoperative outcomes.[20] This is the most instructive architecture cousin for “consultant-not-actor”: an added support layer may filter/escalate information, but it must not be sold as outcome-proven.

**AlertWatch OR — integrated intraoperative data display.** AlertWatch is a named, FDA-cleared multiparameter anesthesia decision-support display that integrates monitor, EHR, and laboratory inputs into an organ-system interface; its alerts have differing priority tiers.[35] The published observational evaluation reported process-of-care improvement but not improved clinical outcomes, and an accompanying editorial highlights the limitations of before/after comparisons.[36-37] It is a useful cousin for a prioritized HUD, but CC should not copy its live-data assumptions, device status, or claims.

**Head-worn displays / AR in anesthesia and airway work — adjacent HUD architecture.** The prior pack covers the primary anesthesia HWD studies. The systematic review by Elrose et al. found limited and sometimes conflicting surrogate/process evidence and no clinical outcomes among the included clinical-context studies.[23] Hart et al.’s 2025 scoping review places AR in airway management in a still-developing evidence base.[24] These systems justify measuring gaze, cognitive load, interruption, and error—not claiming care benefit from a glasses form factor.

### Ambient workflow systems

**Ambient AI scribes — implemented documentation assistance, not real-time crisis AI.** Olson et al. examined ambient AI scribes in relation to administrative burden and professional burnout.[21] Guo et al. qualitatively analyzed what clinicians edited in ambient-AI-drafted documentation.[22] These studies are relevant to ambient audio acquisition, clinician editing, and workflow fit. They are *not* evidence that an ambient stream can safely detect time-critical events, correctly identify an emergency algorithm, or issue action cues. CC should therefore start with constrained simulation audio and explicit silent/uncertain states.

### Retrieval-constrained and specialty-evidence architectures

**Medical retrieval-augmented generation (MedRAG/MIRAGE) — evaluated benchmark architecture, preprint.** Xiong et al. introduced MIRAGE and MedRAG, evaluating 41 retriever/corpus/backbone combinations on 7,663 medical-QA questions. The authors reported benchmark accuracy gains over chain-of-thought prompting, but this is an arXiv preprint/benchmark, not a prospective clinical evaluation.[32] It supports testing retrieval and citations; it does not authorize RAG to generate emergency algorithms.

**Whitelisted retrieval-assisted LLMs — direct architectural analogue.** Masanneck et al. compared web retrieval-assisted LLMs with and without whitelisting for evidence-based neurology.[25] This is the closest retrieved study for source gating: a fixed approved source corpus is an evaluable control, not merely a prompt instruction. Its domain and question-answering setting differ from acute perioperative care, so it supports the *mechanism* of source governance, not safety of CC’s proposed use.

**LLM safety/comparative studies — cautionary evidence, not product validation.** Wang et al. compared approaches intended to improve medical-LLM answer accuracy and safety.[26] Basu and Huynh’s systematic review synthesizes hallucination-mitigation approaches in healthcare AI.[27] Landon et al. found variation in LLM recommendations across challenging inpatient-management scenarios.[30] Together they support the design decision that a general LLM must not author live emergency actions. They do not prove that any one RAG implementation or model is clinically safe.

**OpenEvidence and UpToDate comparisons — specialty evidence systems should be a separate consultant lane.** Hurt et al. reported use of OpenEvidence to augment primary-care decision-making,[28] while Hajj et al. compared ChatGPT-4o and OpenEvidence for structural-heart-disease support.[29] Thiru et al. compared ChatGPT with UpToDate in a preclinical-education semantic-similarity analysis.[31] These are not adequate evidence to crown a particular vendor as an emergency engine, and none justifies using a general conversational model as one. They do support treating curated/specialty evidence retrieval as a distinct, slow consultant function—with citations, scope limits, and no `algorithm_id` authority.

## Contrary / cautionary evidence

1. **Cognitive forcing is not a magic debiasing switch.** Croskerry’s work provides a theory and vocabulary for forcing functions,[7] but systematic reviews of diagnostic checklists/reasoning tools report heterogeneous interventions, settings, and effects.[8-9] A ROUNDS cue should be a timed invitation to reconsider, not a claim that the system can debias the clinician or diagnose the case.

2. **Digital cognitive aids can be ignored or misused.** The retrospective perioperative checklist survey documents that availability does not guarantee use.[6] Cognitive-aid review literature in the prior pack includes simulations with no benefit, slowed diagnosis/treatment, or wrong diagnoses. The UI therefore needs discoverability, an obvious stop/lock function, training, and after-action review—not merely better prompting.

3. **More alerts can be worse.** Repeated alerts, workload, and complexity contribute to alert fatigue.[11] A systematic review found interaction-design and role-tailoring approaches may reduce medication-alert fatigue.[12] Emergency preemption and a quiet `no_supported_match` state are safety features; frequent “maybe” alerts are not.

4. **Human oversight can itself be biased.** Automation-bias systematic reviews describe errors caused by accepting wrong automation or failing to act when an automated system is silent.[13-14] In an experimental study of clinical AI aids, clinicians were susceptible to incorrect recommendations.[15] Faculty lock and clinician override must be quick, salient, and nonpunitive; the HUD must not present uncertainty as a recommendation.

5. **Remote support and highly integrated displays have not shown a general outcome benefit.** ACTFAST’s subsequent randomized outcome study did not find benefit on its tested postoperative outcomes.[20] AlertWatch reports process rather than demonstrated clinical-outcome improvement.[36] These results argue against a “control room makes it safe” narrative.

6. **RAG reduces neither hallucination nor provenance risk to zero.** Benchmark gains and source citations do not guarantee that a retrieved source is applicable, current, locally approved, or correctly synthesized.[25-27,32] Whitelisting must be paired with review/versioning; it cannot be a one-time source list.

7. **Ambient and HUD form factors add interruption, privacy, and attention risks.** Ambient-scribe work measures documentation workflows, not time-critical detection.[21-22] HWD evidence has no demonstrated clinical outcome benefit and identifies limitations of surrogate evidence.[23] Camera-free reduces, but does not resolve, privacy and audio-recognition issues.

## Gaps vs this build

1. **No trial tested this exact dual-mode architecture.** There is no retrieved study of a system that keeps routine cognitive forcing and emergency-manual matching in separate, preemptive catalogs.
2. **No evidence establishes the emergency matcher’s required accuracy.** Before shadow use, CC needs a prospectively specified confusion matrix: supported match, wrong match, delayed match, false emergency, `no_supported_match`, and faculty-lock activation.
3. **No study establishes full-display versus NOW/NEXT/drip superiority.** CC’s full-algorithm requirement is a defensible transparency and recoverability choice, but it remains a design hypothesis to test with simulation usability, time-to-orientation, and error endpoints.
4. **No evidence validates audio-only emergency identification.** Evaluate ambient-noise robustness, overlapping speech, accent/language, speaker distance, latency, and explicit uncertainty handling in simulation. A failed transcription must not become false certainty.
5. **No literature proves a specialty evidence engine can safely act as a real-time emergency engine.** The consultant must stay out of algorithm selection, timing, and action generation until a separately governed evaluation says otherwise.
6. **No patient-outcome evidence supports the glasses layer.** HUD claims should remain limited to usability, workload, gaze, recognition, simulation process adherence, and acceptability until evaluated otherwise.
7. **Local governance remains unsolved.** Each reviewed catalog needs named content owners, version/date, local adaptation record, retirement policy, change-control review, and a visible “not available/not current” state. A frozen PDF alone is not a clinical governance system.
8. **Simulation-to-shadow needs predefined stopping rules.** Examples: any wrong reviewed-algorithm display, safety-critical latency, unacknowledged high-priority cue, or evidence-source mismatch triggers review before broader exposure. These are proposed evaluation safeguards, not validated thresholds.
